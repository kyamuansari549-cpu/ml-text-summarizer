Artificial intelligence is transforming the way software is built and used across every industry. At the heart of this change lies machine learning, a technique that allows computers to learn patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules. The process always begins with data collection, because the quality of the training data largely decides the quality of the final model. Raw data is rarely usable directly, so it goes through cleaning and preprocessing steps such as removing duplicates, handling missing values, and converting text into numerical features. One of the most common ways to turn text into numbers is TF-IDF, which measures how important a word is to a document relative to a whole collection of documents. Words that appear often in one document but rarely elsewhere receive high scores, making them strong signals of what the document is about. In extractive text summarization, these scores are used to rank sentences, and the highest-ranked sentences are stitched together into a summary. Unlike abstractive methods, extractive summarization never invents new wording, so the summary stays faithful to the original text. Evaluation is usually done by comparing the generated summary against human-written reference summaries and measuring word overlap. Although modern transformer models produce more fluent summaries, classical TF-IDF methods remain valuable because they are fast, interpretable, and need no GPU hardware. They are still widely used in news aggregation, legal document review, and research paper skimming. Understanding these fundamentals gives students a solid base before moving on to deep learning approaches.
